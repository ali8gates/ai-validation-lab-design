# Discovery and Problem Framing

How the program got scoped, what the market actually said, and why the framing landed where it did.

## Where the input came from

Three streams fed the work.

**A standing task force.** A cross-functional group covering clinical practice, research acceleration, and ethics. Its job was to decide what the organization should do about AI beyond publishing opinions about it.

**A listening tour.** Conversations across academic centers, community and rural hospitals, payers, regulators, and model developers. The point was to find the shared complaint, not to validate a preferred answer.

**Early technical pilots.** Small collaborative modeling efforts that surfaced the practical constraints early: data access is the bottleneck, governance takes longer than the analysis, and nobody agrees on what a good result looks like.

## What the market said

The same three things came back from nearly every conversation.

**Everyone wants independent evaluation and nobody wants to build it.** Hospitals want proof a tool works on their population. Developers want a credible third party to say so. Both were waiting for someone else to stand it up.

**The capability gap is not evenly distributed.** Large academic centers can run their own evaluation. Community and rural hospitals cannot, and they are the ones most dependent on whatever the vendor hands them.

**Accuracy is the wrong headline.** Buyers asked about total cost, workflow disruption, and what happens when the model is wrong. Nobody led with the performance number, but performance numbers were all the market was producing.

## The three problem statements

Written from each side, because a program that only solves one side does not clear.

**Hospitals, especially outside academic medical centers.** We cannot test a tool before we deploy it. We need an evaluation we can hand to a clinical committee and a finance committee and have both understand it.

**Model developers.** We need third party validation on diverse real world populations. Our own numbers do not move hospital buyers, regulators, or payers, and there is no standard we can point to.

**The organization running the lab.** We need a durable program that fits the mission, uses assets we already have, and holds a defensible position rather than competing with the tools it evaluates.

## Why this organization

Four things had to be true at once, and they rarely are.

- Long running clinical registries and quality improvement programs, so the cohorts and the clinical definitions already exist.
- Existing certification programs, so running a rigorous, repeatable, auditable process at scale is a known capability rather than a new one.
- Guideline authority in the clinical domain, which is what makes a result mean something to a clinician.
- Neutral standing across academia, industry, and community systems. A validation body that sells the tools it evaluates is not a validation body.

## The thesis

Run a neutral, clinically rigorous lab that evaluates cardiovascular AI tools on real world multi-site data, publishes performance and subgroup results in language a clinician and an executive can both act on, and keeps watching after deployment. Make it usable by the hospitals that cannot do this for themselves.

## What was deliberately excluded

- Building or selling models. The moment the lab has a model in the market, its results stop counting.
- Acting as a regulator. The lab produces evidence. It does not approve or clear anything.
- Guaranteeing outcomes. A validation says how a model performed on a defined population under a defined protocol. It is not a warranty.
- Evaluating everything. Cardiovascular and stroke first, where the clinical depth is real. Everything else is a later question.

## Open questions carried forward

- What the minimum evidence bar is for a favorable result, and who owns changing it.
- How a mark or seal is granted, revoked, and time-limited, since an unexpiring seal on a drifting model is a liability.
- How to keep the funded work aimed at under-resourced hospitals rather than drifting toward whoever pays the most.
- How to handle a developer who does not like the result. That policy has to be written before the first unfavorable one, not after.
